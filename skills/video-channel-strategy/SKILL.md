# Video Channel Strategy (worksheet)

A channel is a long bet on one viewer's trust. Answer these slots in order, with the person, before anyone writes a script. Each slot has a question, a way to decide, and a blank for the answer. Fill every blank in a private copy of this worksheet; keep this public file generic.

Evidence tiers used below: **sourced** (named source, dated), **observed** (seen in how established channels work, not measured), **judgment** (reasoning from the person's situation, a proposal). Mark every answer with its tier. Third-party growth statistics from creator-tool vendors and case-study blogs are marketing material: treat them as directional.

## 0. Collect inputs first

Ask for, or read from the repo: assets (code, writing, research, hardware), what the person can prove in public, what is under contract or NDA, how they feel about performing (reading, memorized, off the cuff), time per week, and what they dislike making. Do not start from the archetype; start from this list.

`inputs: {{assets}} / {{provable}} / {{restricted}} / {{performing comfort}} / {{hours per week}} / {{dread list}}`

## 1. Goal and destination

Question: what is the channel for? Pick one: reach and credibility with peers, search-led education for a service business, a local network, or entertainment. Mixing them dilutes the channel.

Decide by: what the person wants in two years that a viewer could give them (invitations, hiring, inbound work, sponsorships, an audience of their own). If they say credibility but already have it, the job is narrative and retention, not credentials: a stranger's first question is whether the video is worth watching.

Once the goal is set, do not re-rank lanes by what the person sells. Pointing a reach channel at the sales offer swaps knowledge only they have for topics anyone could cover. Retention comes from that rare knowledge, and so does sponsor and inbound value.

`goal: {{one goal}}` · `two-year outcome: {{outcome}}` · `success measured by: {{retention curve, subscribers, inbound}}`

## 2. Lane

Question: what will the first 20 videos be about? A broad range is an advantage in conversation and a handicap on a channel. A recognizable channel has one lane.

Score each candidate lane 1 to 5 on four criteria, and keep the top one or two:

1. **Can show**: real first-hand evidence on screen.
2. **Can say**: public sources exist, nothing restricted is needed.
3. **Wants to make 50 of**: the person would not dread it. Topics tied to a dreaded subject (for example regulatory minutiae) fail here even when the person is expert.
4. **Gap**: few existing channels cover it well. Check by searching, not by memory.

Look for a **unifying property** across the person's domains. A lane defined by a property ("software where a mistake is expensive") can hold work from several sectors and keeps each sector as texture, not as the subject.

`candidates: {{lane}}: can show {{n}}, can say {{n}}, wants {{n}}, gap {{n}}` · `chosen lane: {{lane}}` · `unifying property: {{property}}`

## 3. Archetype and persona

Common archetypes:

| Archetype | Credibility comes from | Needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curious explorer (script-led) | Depth of research, clarity | Strong writing, steady voice over a script |
| Practical operator (field-led) | Demonstrated results, visible method | Presence on camera, improvising, gear, space |
| Closed-world reporter | Access plus restraint: knows what not to say | Rules for sourcing, safety, attribution |

When a person lists candidate identities ("the X guy"), sort each one first: a **format** (how they teach: visual explainer, technician, tour), a **lane** (what the videos are about), or a **job title** (what they sell). Only a lane plus a stance answers "the X guy". Job titles belong to the sales properties; formats become series inside the lane. A loaded word in a handle (hacker, insider) works when it names a way of looking at things, not a job the person would have to prove.

Choose by strengths, not by which looks bigger. If answers split, start script-led; a script can be re-recorded, and field footage can be added later. A **persona is a constraint list**, not a costume:

- stance in one sentence
- what the character does not know, and says so
- three to five recurring questions asked of every topic (the lens)
- banned moves (for example fake urgency, outrage, ranking, a title the video cannot deliver)

`archetype: {{archetype}}` · `stance: {{sentence}}` · `lens: {{3 to 5 questions}}` · `banned: {{list}}`

## 4. Credential and backstory ledger

Make a table before any script. Each row is one claim the persona might make.

| Claim | Evidence in hand | Allowed phrasing | Forbidden phrasing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {{claim}} | {{document, public record, nothing}} | {{exact words}} | {{implied versions}} |

Rules:

- A mask is a voice, not a false résumé. Never state or imply a credential, employer, clearance, award or service you cannot document.
- Where the person lacks a clearance, licence or title, use it as the stance: "public sources only, nothing restricted", and name the sources on screen. The absence of access is a reason to trust the sourcing, not a gap to hide.
- Work under contract or NDA: describe the problem class and the lesson, never the client or product. Read the contract for publication or pre-review clauses before telling any story from it.
- The person's own account of their work and accomplishments is the source. Record it in the ledger as they state it and do not reopen it; repeated "please confirm" items read as distrust. Agents never supply numbers about past work themselves.
- A personal story that carries risk (a dismissal, a dispute) gets one agreed true line or is omitted. A teaser with no payoff reads as hiding something.
- If the person has already ruled out a subject for their brand (a past sector, a failed niche), ask whether an anecdote counts. A rule about positioning does not always cover a story.

## 5. Handle and brand architecture

Question: what is the name, and how does the channel relate to the person's other properties?

- Keep one identity line and one name string everywhere. The persona is a voice, not a new job title (see `portfolio-cohesion-audit`, `brand-identity`).
- Decide the handle against two risks: association (does it invite a reading the person does not want) and exposure (a real name plus sensitive topics invites harassment).
- The channel is its own front door. Other businesses appear only when they are the subject of a video or a prop on screen, never as a pitch in the body. Description footer: the identity line and the hub link. Hub and channel link to each other and the channel is listed in the hub's `sameAs`.

`handle: {{handle}}` · `identity line: {{line}}` · `risks accepted: {{list}}` · `business appearances allowed: {{list}}`

## 6. Format and delivery

- Long-form deep dives are the base. Short clips show the result, withhold the method and point to the full video. Test the ratio; treat any suggested long-to-short split as a rough unverified rule of thumb.
- Delivery options: read a script, memorize and deliver it semi-live, or bullets. Test one recording and watch it back; memorization that shows sounds flat.
- Fix one setup (background, lighting, framing) and reuse it.
- Lead with the payoff or the question, not an introduction.

`delivery: {{choice}}` · `test recording done: {{date}}` · `ratio: {{long/short}}`

## 7. Sources and evidence rules

Rank sources by how hard they are to copy: the person's own code and experiments; primary documents (papers, standards, directives, court filings, postmortems, government reports); historical cases; secondary summaries only to find primaries. The video must add something the source does not: a reproduction, a comparison, a place where it is wrong or silent. Publish a small repository or notes per video, named for it. Date and tier every third-party claim.

`first primary sources: {{list with links and dates}}`

## 8. Risk screen (run per topic)

- Restricted material: could this touch anything under contract, NDA, classification or a licence condition? If unsure, drop it.
- Sensitive communities (underground forums, security research): observe, do not participate; public pages only; no links, handles, instructions or names of individuals; attribute exactly ("a Russian-language forum", not "Russian hackers", unless sourced); record on a separate machine with no personal accounts.
- Platform policy: check the current rules for hacking, dark-web, weapons and medical topics before publishing. State that it was checked and when.
- Harm: could an instruction in the video be followed to hurt someone?
- Legal: statements about identifiable people or companies need a source.

`topic screen: {{pass/fail, reason, date}}`

## 9. Pipeline, first-three test and review

1. Backlog of 20 titles scored on "adds something original" and "fits the lens"; delete the rest.
2. Research notes with each claim tiered.
3. Script for the ear, read aloud, with a second reader checking claims.
4. Publish with sources and repository in the description.
5. Record three videos, one repository each. Order them by strength of evidence, strongest first.
6. Review after six weeks on retention curves, not view counts. Fix the next opening where viewers left. Write the continue, change or stop rule before the first upload.

Growth is slow in technical niches: treat any figure for time to a subscriber milestone as a rough unverified rule of thumb. Plan for 20 to 30 videos before the data means anything.

`first three: {{titles}}` · `review date: {{date}}` · `stop rule: {{rule}}`

## 10. Research the slot with data

When filling slot 2 or 3, run searches rather than relying on memory: comparable channels and their format, audience size, what the best-performing videos have in common, and whether the lane is crowded. Cite the source and date. A claim of "this is covered territory" needs a named example.

## Output

A filled copy of this worksheet saved somewhere private, each answer marked decided or proposed, open items listed, and nothing treated as decided until the person confirms it. The private copy holds only what is current: when the person changes a decision, rewrite that slot in the same turn and delete the old answer and any open item it settles. A stale proposal left in the file is read as the plan by the next agent.
