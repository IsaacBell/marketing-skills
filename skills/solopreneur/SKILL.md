# Solopreneur Portfolio Triage

For a solo (often neurodivergent, often ADHD-shaped) founder who has built more than one real thing — a consulting site, a product, a side domain, an open-source repo, a newsletter — and is stuck converting any of them into income. The failure mode isn't lack of output. It's too much parallel output with no single property forced through to a sale.

## When to use this vs. other skills

- One specific site needs SEO work → `seo-audit`, `seo-project-setup`.
- One specific piece of copy needs writing → `copy-safety-review`.
- The user needs a positioning/voice system for ONE brand → `voice-and-positioning`.
- The user has 2+ properties and doesn't know which to push, or wants to know if they read as one coherent person/brand across all of them → this skill.

## Step 1 — Inventory, don't audit yet

List every live property the user owns: domains, GitHub profile, social profiles, apps. For each, capture in one line: what it sells, who it's for, how much traffic/inbound it has, and its current price point/ticket size. Don't skip a property just because the user calls it a "side thought" — an unbuilt domain is still an inventory line (status: idea, not yet a property).

## Step 2 — Read each property as a stranger would

Visit each live URL. Extract: headline claim, who it's obviously for, price signal (cheap/mid/high-ticket), and call to action. Do this before forming an opinion — don't rely on the user's own description of a property, which is usually stale (sites get worked on between conversations).

## Step 3 — Find the altitude mismatch

The usual diagnosis isn't "your work is bad," it's that the properties sit at different altitudes and don't explain each other:

- High-ticket enterprise/compliance consulting next to budget small-business builds next to unlaunched side bets next to an open-source tools profile — each individually coherent, collectively confusing to a first-time visitor who lands on just one.
- Name this explicitly: which property is the "main" one, which are proof-of-work, which are dead weight that should be sold, killed, or hidden until they're real.

## Step 4 — Pick ONE revenue lane, not a rebalance of all of them

A solo founder with 3+ properties and zero income from any of them does not need all of them fixed in parallel. Recommend the single property with the shortest path from current state to a paid transaction at the founder's current rates — usually: most finished, most concrete existing proof (a live client build, a case study), not the one with the biggest theoretical upside. Do not pick an offer because it is the cheapest rung. A price the founder has raised or called a floor is a decision, not an opening bid. Say which one and why, in one paragraph, not a scored matrix.

## Step 5 — Match the hire to the actual gap, not the trendiest title

Don't default to "growth hacker" or "OBM" because they sound senior. Ask what's missing:

- No cohesive story across properties, no idea how you come across → an outside identity/positioning review (cheap, fast, high leverage — often the actual next step before spending on anything else).
- A finished offer with no buyers yet → a marketer/GTM generalist who can run one channel end-to-end.
- Real operational volume (calls, leads, fulfillment) already happening → an OBM.
- An ad budget and a proven funnel → a growth hacker/paid-acquisition specialist.

Most solo founders with no traction yet are pre-OBM and pre-growth-hacker. Say so plainly even if it's not the title the user suggested.

## Step 6 — Write the ask (Upwork/contractor post) from real inventory, not vibes

The post should name: the properties, the one lane being pushed, the specific deliverable wanted (e.g., a written identity/cohesion review with before/after fixes — not "help me with marketing"), and a real budget range. Neurodivergent-founder framing is fine as color but the ask itself must be scoped enough that a freelancer can quote it in one read.

## Step 7 — Get the chosen lane its first customers

The constraint is founder hours, not budget. One person runs two channels well, never five.

- **First 5-10 buyers**: personal messages to specific people or businesses with the problem, naming their situation. Warm network and past clients first. No blasts. Naming their situation means quoting what they wrote (a job post, a launch note) and proposing the first move. A message that asks them to explain their own need shows you did not read it.
- **Next batch**: one or two communities or marketplaces where the buyer already shows up (a niche subreddit, a Discord, Etsy/Fiverr/Upwork for services). Participate before pitching. A niche venue beats a broad feed.
- **Double down before diversifying**: when one channel produces replies or sales, put the next month into it. Add a second channel only after the first converts on repeat.
- **Offer readiness test**: one sentence that names who it's for and what it does, a visible price, and one piece of proof (a shipped build, a screenshot, a client quote). Missing any of the three → fix that before outreach.
- **Charge from day one**: a payment button or quote beats a waitlist. Usage without payment proves curiosity, not a business. A time-boxed launch discount is a bridge, not the pricing model.
- **Build in public only if sustainable**: weekly floor, mostly lessons and real numbers, rare direct promotion, replies count as the work. Skip it if the founder can't keep the cadence; a dead feed is worse than none.
- **Sell through the founder's strong medium**: before building a plan around a format the founder must perform (live calls, interviews, video), check they can and want to. If live calls go badly, sell in writing: the ask is a reply, the reply is a one-page written plan, and any call walks through that plan. A recurring show the founder finds boring will not survive the first month. Measure signed work, not booked calls: a booked call is worth nothing to a founder who cannot close on one.
- **Platform risk**: a marketplace (Etsy, eBay, Fiverr) is fine for first sales, but route repeat buyers to an owned channel (email, own site). Account holds, KYC blocks, and algorithm changes are single points of failure.

## When the founder delivers through contractors

Many "solo" founders sell alone but deliver with contractors. The founder scopes and sells; a team builds. That changes three steps above:

- **Step 4, the lane.** The price has to cover contractor cost plus a margin. A low rung that barely pays the founder often pays nobody once a contractor delivers it. Prefer the offer the team can deliver at a real margin, even when the ticket is larger. One larger build that keeps the team working for weeks beats several small jobs that each need the founder to scope and sell.
- **Step 5, the hire.** Delivery capacity already exists, so the gap is the pipeline, and the founder owns it. Do not recommend another delivery hire before the pipeline is full.
- **Step 7, the offer.** A team is part of the offer: the buyer gets more than one person's hours and is not stuck if that person is unavailable. Write "we" only when a team really delivers the work. Founder hours still cap selling, because contractors do not sell, so the two-channel limit still holds.

## Guardrails

- Decisions the founder has already recorded (prices, offers, rejected angles, voice) outrank this skill. Find them before recommending, and say when this skill's advice conflicts with one.
- When a founder has cycled through titles for years with no income, check whether the original title had the most proof. It often did. Narrow it with a qualifier taken from current demand (quote real job posts), not with a new title. A new title does not fix missing distribution.
- Separate hiring processes from sales processes. A founder who was hired through interviews many times but has never closed a sales call should aim at hiring processes: roles, contracts, written applications. Interviews test the work; sales calls sell the person.
- Check what the founder already tried before proposing a niche. Old titles on the live properties and in earlier sessions show it. A niche they tried and dropped is a rejection, not an opportunity.
- Don't recommend fixing all properties at once. Multi-front effort is the actual failure mode being diagnosed.
- Don't let "side thought" projects (an unbuilt domain, a rename idea) consume the same planning weight as the property that's closest to revenue.
- Ground every claim in what you observed on the live site/profile, not the user's memory of it — memory of your own stuff is reliably stale.
- If asked to also produce content (checklists, case studies, lead magnets) as proof for the chosen lane, base it on the user's real, provable work history — commits, launched projects, actual client outcomes — not generic best-practice filler. Verbose, unspecific advice is the exact failure this skill exists to catch.
