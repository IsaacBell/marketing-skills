# Tech Content Marketing

## Goal

Be findable and credible at the moment a technical buyer is researching, so that by the time they contact you they already trust the product. The work is original educational content that ranks in search and gets cited in AI answers, plus a way for readers to act without talking to a salesperson. Promotion follows usefulness; it is not the point of the article.

## When to use this vs other skills

- **seo-audit**: the site itself is the problem (crawl errors, slow mobile pages, missing metadata, indexing failures). Fix that first; content on a broken site underperforms.
- **answer-engine-optimization**: the job is being named or quoted inside AI-generated answers.
- **copywriting**: the words on a page, once the topic and reader are decided.
- **This skill**: deciding what to publish, for which reader, and how it connects to revenue.

## Audience

Three groups behave differently and need different content.

- **Developers** evaluate by using. They rarely hold budget but carry strong influence. They want documentation, working examples, a free tier and value within minutes.
- **Technical buyers** (architects, platform, security and data leads) shortlist and compare. They read comparisons, migration guides and operational detail, and weigh integration, security and running cost. Most content should target them.
- **Executives** approve spend at the end. They want risk, cost and business outcome. Keep their material short and separate.

Benefit language loses developers. Feature-only language loses executives. Do not put both in one piece.

## Content types that work, and why

- **Long-form technical guides** built from keyword research. This is the base that ranks and gets cited. Code, diagrams and tables hold attention.
- **Comparison and alternative pages.** Buyers ask how two tools differ; whoever publishes the credible comparison shapes the shortlist.
- **Tutorials and migration guides.** High intent, and they prove you understand the work.
- **Concept explainers** on the underlying technology, with no pitch. They earn trust and links.
- **Category roundups** for "best tools for X" queries. Include competitors honestly or readers dismiss it.
- **Case studies** with real numbers, named outcomes and screenshots.
- **Product pages** built on the top three differentiators, stated as features.
- **A short product demo** showing the actual interface.
- **Reference docs and changelogs.** Public documentation is marketing surface too.

Technical readers spot padding, generic advice and unreviewed AI drafts within seconds. Depth and first-hand experience are the only durable advantage.

## Developer marketing specifics

- Put someone with engineering experience on the marketing side to translate between product, community and marketing.
- Subject experts author; editors structure and tighten.
- Be useful in community forums for months before linking a product.
- Offer a free tier or open source with time to value under ten minutes. Do not open with a demo request.
- Keep product documentation public, even though competitors read it.
- Sponsor narrow, trusted newsletters instead of broad ad networks.
- Reach out personally and specifically, from a technical person. Skip automated drip sequences.
- A developer liking the product starts the sale. They still have to convince the rest of the organization, so give them material to forward.

## Distribution

The long-form article lives on your own site. Then:

- Summarize it into social posts that link back.
- Write distinct versions for third-party publications. Never repost the same text, even with a canonical tag.
- Cut one article into smaller pieces: a newsletter note, a docs snippet, a short video.
- Place contributed pieces where your readers already go, not where marketing content usually lands.

## Measuring success

Track search impressions and clicks, engaged visits, free-tier signups, demo requests, content-influenced pipeline, branded search volume and AI citations. An article often takes months to peak and decays slowly after that.

Per article, ask one question: did it get seen, and did anyone act? Merge or retire posts that never gain impressions.

Ignore raw pageviews without engagement, week-to-week ranking swings, unqualified lead counts and daily rank checks. Do not judge the program on one month.

## Working with an agency or writers

Test expertise before signing:

- Ask them how a buyer in your market searches, and what one specific keyword means to that buyer.
- Ask what they would write first and why, then check the reasoning against your product.
- Read samples on topics as technical as yours. Look for code, tests and honesty about limits.
- Good sign: they ask to talk to your engineers. Bad sign: samples full of benefit claims with no specifics.

Keep in-house: positioning, product pages, case studies, anything that needs roadmap knowledge. Outsource: keyword research, drafting under engineer review, technical SEO fixes, ads. Do not pull engineers into bulk writing; their time is worth more on the product.

## Writing the piece

One claim per sentence, average about 20 words and a hard maximum of 35. Vary sentence length; three or more short sentences in a row read as staccato, so merge them. Do not open three consecutive sentences with the same subject. Keep the article ('the context window', not 'context window'). Replace an adjective with a number where one exists. One term per concept for the whole piece, defined at first use, never rotated synonyms. Read the section aloud and rewrite anything you would not say to a colleague.

Date every third-party claim next to the claim: version, price, limit, benchmark. Vendor behavior is perishable, so an undated claim goes false without anyone editing the text. State the date the value was observed, and recheck each dated claim when the piece is refreshed.

Define evidence tiers where a piece mixes them: measured (reproduced from a named script or repo), sourced (traced to a named primary source), estimated (judgment, labeled with what it depends on). Keep hypotheses labeled until repeated evidence supports them, and carry known weaknesses forward instead of dropping them. Say which part of the piece is fact and which is proposal or opinion.

Changelog entries state user-visible impact in the present tense and record removals, not only additions. Group related changes and never duplicate an existing entry. A breaking change leads the entry and names the migration action. Keep release notes as the audience-facing cut of the same facts; the two must never contradict each other.

## Checklist before publishing

- One target keyword, with real search demand and clear intent.
- Someone with hands-on experience reviewed it.
- Each section answers its heading in the first sentence or two.
- Original evidence: code, screenshots, test results or a stated opinion.
- The product appears once, in a callout, not woven through the text.
- One call to action for early-stage readers and one for readers ready to buy.
- Internal links to related posts, external links to sources.
- Title and description set; page fast on mobile.
- Publish date visible, with a plan to refresh the piece.

## Guardrails

- No performance claims you cannot demonstrate.
- No competitor claims you have not tested yourself.
- No bought links, keyword stuffing or duplicate posting.
- No AI drafts published without expert review.
- No promises the product cannot keep.
